Chapter 10: Permutations and Combinations

Six lessons on counting: from the multiplication principle and factorial notation to permutations, combinations, restrictions and the mixed problems that combine them.

Counting every possibility, without listing them

Permutations and combinations are the mathematics of counting without counting. Instead of writing out every possible arrangement, you use a formula to find how many there are.

Permutations and combinations are the tools you use whenever a problem asks “how many ways?” — how many ways to arrange books on a shelf, choose a team from a class, or form a password from a set of letters. The two ideas are closely related: a permutation counts arrangements where order matters, and a combination counts selections where order does not. Getting the distinction right is the single most important skill in this chapter.

The lessons are arranged so that each idea builds on the last. Sections 10.1 and 10.2 establish the multiplication principle and factorial notation — the two pieces of machinery that every formula in the chapter depends on. Section 10.3 introduces the permutation formula nPr and the reasoning behind it, and Section 10.4 shows how to handle the restrictions that appear in exam questions, such as items that must sit together or repeated letters in a word.

Sections 10.5 and 10.6 move on to combinations and to the mixed problems that combine selection with arrangement. By the end you will be able to look at a counting problem, decide whether order matters, choose between nPr and nCr, and break the problem into stages without double-counting. Each section includes worked examples with every line explained, followed by practice problems that check your answer immediately.

If you are revising for an exam, work through the sections in order the first time, then use the cards below to jump straight back to whichever technique you need to refresh. The final section on mixed problems is the one most likely to appear in an unfamiliar form on a test, so give it extra practice once the earlier sections feel comfortable.

What you will learn

  • The multiplication principle for staged decisions
  • Factorial notation and the value of n!
  • Permutations: nPr = n! / (n − r)! for ordered arrangements
  • Permutations with restrictions: items together, items separated, identical items
  • Combinations: nCr = n! / [r!(n − r)!] for selections
  • Mixed problems that combine choosing and arranging
  • How counting underpins simple probability

Prerequisite: basic algebra, including fractions and simplifying expressions, and the idea of a probability from earlier years.

Lessons in this chapter

Work through them in order, or jump to the section you need. Each lesson includes a short explanation, worked examples and practice.

10.1 The multiplication principle

Count the number of ways several independent choices can be made, and use tree diagrams to see why the counts multiply. This is the idea every later formula rests on.
Counting10 min

10.2 Factorial notation

Understand n! as the number of ways to arrange n distinct objects, then simplify expressions involving factorials and use them in counting problems.
Counting12 min

10.3 Permutations of distinct objects

Use the permutation formula to count ordered arrangements of r objects chosen from n, and learn to recognise when order matters in a problem.
Permutations15 min

10.4 Permutations with restrictions

Handle arrangements where certain items must sit together, must be kept apart, or are identical, by treating groups as single objects or dividing out repeats.
